# Evidence log: how much strength training does a distance runner need?

## Questions

1. What strength-training methods (load, sets, reps, frequency, duration) improve running economy or
   running performance in middle- and long-distance runners?
2. Is there a dose-response between weekly sets and strength gain, and what is the minimum effective dose?
3. What is the minimum dose to *maintain* strength when training is reduced?
4. Does strength training prevent injuries (in sport generally, and in runners)?

## Search

Europe PMC, titles containing "strength training" with "running economy" / "distance runners" /
"endurance runners" and "systematic review" / "meta-analysis", sorted by citations; titles on weekly set
volume / minimum effective / minimal dose / maintaining performance; titles on strength training + injury
in runners. Inclusion: systematic reviews/meta-analyses and RCTs; position stands for prescription norms.

## Sources

### S1. Llanos-Lagos C, et al. Sports Med 2024;54:895–932 (PMC11052887) — FT
"Effect of Strength Training Programs in Middle- and Long-Distance Runners' Economy at Different Running
Speeds: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis." 31 studies, 652 runners (moderately to highly trained),
programmes 6–24 weeks, 1–4 sessions/week. GRADE certainty moderate (high-load, submaximal, plyometric,
isometric) and low (combined).
- High-load training (HL, ≥ 80% 1RM or ≤ 7RM): "small improvement on RE (ES = −0.266, p = 0.039)" at
  8.64–17.85 km/h; "no moderating effect of duration in weeks, sessions per week or total sessions on RE
  (all p > 0.111)". Moderator analysis: greater effect at higher speeds and higher VO2max.
- Combined methods (two or more of HL / submaximal / plyometric): moderate effect (ES −0.426 after removing
  outliers, p = 0.018) at 10.00–14.45 km/h.
- Submaximal-load training (40–79% 1RM) alone: "no significant effect … (p = 0.131)" (only 3 studies).
- Isometric training alone: "no significant effect … (p = 0.253)" (3 studies, all ankle plantarflexion).
- Plyometric training alone: no significant main effect (p = 0.167), but in sub-group analysis "PL had a
  beneficial effect when the speeds are less or equal to 12.00 km/h" (β1 = 0.47, p = 0.017); the abstract
  reports a small effect (ES −0.307) at ≤ 12.00 km/h.
- Limits: few studies per method; most measured economy at absolute speeds; populations mostly
  moderately-to-highly trained, 17–45 y.
- Note for this runner: easy pace ≈ 10:30 /mi ≈ 9.2 km/h, marathon pace 10:18 /mi ≈ 9.4 km/h — inside
  the ≤ 12 km/h range where the plyometric sub-group effect was found, and at the low end of the HL range.

### S2. Llanos-Lagos C, et al. Sports Med 2024;54:1801–1833 (PMC11258194) — FT (abstract/key points)
"The Effect of Strength Training Methods on Middle-Distance and Long-Distance Runners' Athletic
Performance." 38 studies, 894 runners, 6–40 weeks, 1–4 sessions/week; certainty very low to moderate.
- HL: "moderate (effect size = −0.469, p = 0.029)" improvement in running performance (time trial / time
  to exhaustion); combined methods: "large effect (effect size = −1.035, p = 0.036)"; plyometric alone
  "not … significant (effect size = −0.210, p = 0.064)".
- "None of the training methods improved VO2max, vVO2max, MMSS, or sprint capacity."
- Moderators including weeks, sessions per week and total sessions "had no effect" (all p > 0.166).

### S3. Balsalobre-Fernández C, et al. J Strength Cond Res 2016;30:2361–2368 — ABS
Five controlled trials, 93 high-level runners (VO2max > 60). Programmes used "2-4 resistance lower-body
exercises plus up to 200 jumps and 5-10 short sprints) 2-3 times per week for 8-12 weeks"; large
beneficial effect on running economy (SMD −1.42). Limit: highly trained runners only; 5 studies.

### S4. Trowell D, et al. Sports Med 2020;50:133–150 — ABS
25 studies. Concurrent strength and endurance training "improves the force-generating capacity of the
ankle plantarflexors, quadriceps, hamstrings and gluteal muscles", but "there is no evidence to suggest
these adaptations transfer from strength exercises to running" biomechanics.

### S5. Ralston GW, et al. Sports Med 2017;47:2585–2601 (PMC5684266) — FT
"The Effect of Weekly Set Volume on Strength Gain: A Meta-Analysis." Nine studies (general trainees,
mostly young men; not runners). Weekly sets **per exercise**: low ≤ 5, medium 5–9, high ≥ 10.
- High vs low weekly sets: greater strength gain (ES difference 0.18, 95% CI 0.06–0.30, p = 0.003),
  described by the authors as a "trivial effect"; medium vs low: "marginally greater" (ES 0.15, p = 0.04).
- Conclusion: "a graded dose-response relationship between weekly sets performed and strength gain"; low
  weekly sets still produced strength gains (mean pre–post ES for low weekly sets 0.82).

### S6. Androulakis-Korakakis P, et al. Sports Med 2020;50:751–765 — ABS
Resistance-trained men. "A single set performed minimum 1 time and maximum 3 times per week was sufficient
to induce significant 1RM strength gains"; "a single set of 6-12 repetitions with loads ranging from
approximately 70-85% 1RM 2-3 times per week with high intensity of effort (reaching volitional or momentary
failure) for 8-12 weeks can produce suboptimal, yet significant increases" in squat and bench press 1RM.

### S7. Nuzzo JL, et al. Sports Med 2024;54:1139–1162 (PMC11127831) — FT available (abstract quoted)
Overview of minimal-dose strategies in people not currently strength training. "'Weekend Warrior' and
single-set resistance exercise are the approaches most strongly supported by current research"; "All
approaches increase muscle strength".

### S8. Spiering BA, et al. J Strength Cond Res 2021;35:1449–1458 — ABS (narrative review)
"Strength and muscle size (at least in younger populations) can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with as
little as 1 session of strength training per week and 1 set per exercise, as long as exercise intensity
(relative load) is maintained." "Insufficient data exists to make specific recommendations for athletes."

### S9. ACSM position stand. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2009;41:687–708 — ABS (guideline)
Novice: loads of 8–12 RM, 2–3 days/week. Intermediate/advanced: 1–12 RM periodized "with eventual emphasis
on heavy loading (1-6 RM) using 3- to 5-min rest periods". Progression: "2-10% increase in load … when the
individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number."
Sequence "multiple-joint exercises before single-joint exercises". Local muscular endurance: 40–60% 1RM,
> 15 reps, < 90 s rest.

### S10. Lauersen JB, et al. Br J Sports Med 2018;52:1557–1563 — ABS
Six RCTs, 7738 participants (sport generally): strength training RR 0.338 (0.238–0.480) for injury; "A 10%
increase in strength training volume reduced the risk of injury by more than four percentage points."

### S11. Toresdahl BG, et al. Sports Health 2020;12:74–79 (PMC6931177) — ABS
RCT, 720 first-time NYC marathoners; a self-directed 10-minute programme 3×/week (quadriceps, hip
abductors, core) for 12 weeks: overuse injury causing non-completion 7.1% vs 7.3% (RR 0.97, 95% CI
0.57–1.63); finish times not different.

### S12. Štohanzl M, et al. J Sports Med Phys Fitness 2018;58:1211–1217 — ABS
RCT, 41 female recreational runners, 10 weeks: 30 or 60 min/week of strength training (replacing some
running) improved maximal treadmill test performance; no change in running economy or VO2max.

## What the evidence does and doesn't say (no decisions here)

- Strength training benefits for runners' economy/performance come mainly from **high loads (≥ 80% 1RM /
  ≤ ~7RM)** and **combined methods** (heavy + plyometric/submaximal). Submaximal-only and isometric-only
  programmes did not show significant economy effects (few studies).
- Frequency in the runner studies was 1–4 sessions/week (mostly 2–3) for 6–24+ weeks; within that range,
  sessions/week and duration did not significantly moderate the effects (S1, S2).
- More weekly sets give somewhat more strength (S5), but single sets 1–3×/week still produce meaningful
  strength gains when effort is high (S6, S7). One session/week of 1 set per exercise at maintained load
  maintains strength for months (S8).
- Injury prevention: strong for strength training across sports (S10), but a low-dose, self-directed
  programme did not reduce injuries in first-time marathoners (S11).
- Not found: a trial testing different weekly-set doses specifically in recreational marathon runners.
