# Evidence log: how to tell an easy run is easy (pace, talk test, heart rate)

## Context

The plan sets easy pace at 11:45–13:00 /mi (from Daniels' VDOT tables, a coaching method). The runner's
easy runs come out near 10:30 /mi and running slower feels hard. They run with a Garmin watch (wrist heart
rate) and can report average heart rate. Goal marathon pace is 10:18 /mi.

## Questions

1. What objective boundary separates "easy" (low-intensity) running from harder running?
2. Does the talk test (or RPE) locate that boundary?
3. Can heart rate be used, and are fixed %HRmax cut-offs reliable for an individual?
4. Is a wrist-worn optical heart-rate monitor accurate enough while running?
5. Why does keeping most running easy matter?

## Search

Europe PMC: "talk test" (by citations); heart rate vs ventilatory/lactate thresholds in runners; wrist /
optical / Garmin heart-rate validity reviews; training-intensity distribution reviews in runners; age-
predicted maximal heart rate.

## Sources

### E1. Persinger R, Foster C, et al. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2004;36:1632–1636 — ABS
16 volunteers, treadmill and cycle: "The Talk Test approximates ventilatory threshold"; "At the point where
speech first became difficult, exercise intensity was almost exactly equivalent to ventilatory threshold.
When speech was not comfortable, exercise intensity was consistently above ventilatory threshold." On the
treadmill, ventilatory threshold occurred at **89% of HRpeak** on average; the equivocal talk-test stage at
86% HRpeak.

### E2. Reed JL, Pipe AL. Curr Opin Cardiol 2014;29:475–480 — ABS (review)
"comfortable speech is likely possible … when exercise intensity is below the ventilatory or lactate
threshold, and not likely possible … when exercise intensity exceeds" it; "valid, reliable, practical and
inexpensive … in competitive athletes, healthy active adults and patients with cardiovascular disease";
consistent across walking, jogging, cycling and other modes.

### E3. Bok D, Rakovac M, Foster C. Sports Med 2022;52:2085–2109 — ABS (review)
"The TT and RPE appear to be valid measures of both the ventilatory threshold and the respiratory
compensation threshold." Equivocal talk-test stage and RPE 10–11 (6–20 Borg scale) reflect the
ventilatory threshold; negative talk-test stage and RPE 13–15 reflect the respiratory compensation
threshold.

### E4. Quinn TJ, Coons BA. J Sports Sci 2011;29:1175–1182 — ABS
15 participants: at the last comfortable-speech stage they were at 82 ± 7% HRmax; at the equivocal stage
90 ± 6% HRmax. Talk-test stages related more closely to the lactate threshold than the ventilatory
threshold in this sample. (Shows individual spread and that thresholds sit at a high %HRmax.)

### E5. Esteve-Lanao J, Foster C, Seiler S, Lucia A. J Strength Cond Res 2007;21:943–949 — ABS (RCT)
12 sub-elite runners, 5 months, zones set from each runner's ventilatory and respiratory compensation
thresholds and controlled by heart rate: ~80% of time below the ventilatory threshold improved 10.4-km
performance more (−157 s) than ~67% (−121.5 s).

### E6. Campos Y, et al. Int J Sports Med 2022;43:305–316 — ABS (systematic review, 20 studies)
Pyramidal/polarized distributions with "high-volume at low-intensity (≥ 70% of overall training volume)"
improved endurance performance more than threshold-focused training; different quantification methods
(session RPE, heart rate, lactate, race pace, speed) classify the same training differently.

### E7. Kenneally M, et al. Int J Sports Physiol Perform 2018;13:1114–1121 — ABS (systematic review)
"pyramidal and polarized training are more effective than threshold training".

### E8. Tanaka H, Monahan KD, Seals DR. J Am Coll Cardiol 2001;37:153–156 — ABS
Age-predicted HRmax = 208 − 0.7 × age (meta-analysis of 18,712 people, cross-validated). A population
average; individuals vary (the abstract does not give the individual error).

### E9. Zhang Y, et al. J Sports Sci 2020;38:2021–2034 — ABS (systematic review + meta-analysis)
44 studies, 15 brands of wrist-worn optical HR devices vs ECG/chest strap: mean difference −0.51 bpm
during treadmill walking-to-running; larger errors during resistance training (−7.26 bpm) and cycling.
"acceptable validity … for most common activities."

### E10. Støve MP, et al. J Sports Sci 2019;37:895–901 — ABS
Garmin Forerunner 235 vs chest-strap: high agreement during treadmill running at 8.7 km/h (r = 0.906) and
12.1 km/h (r = 0.845); low agreement during slow walking; "measurement latency may potentially affect
application."

## What the evidence does and doesn't say (no decisions here)

- The objective "easy" boundary is the first ventilatory threshold; the talk test tracks it well: if you can
  speak comfortably in full sentences you are below it (E1–E3). Effort ~RPE 10–11 on the 6–20 scale is at
  that boundary (E3).
- That threshold sits at a high and individual %HRmax (≈ 82–90% HRmax in E1/E4), so a fixed "easy = below X%
  HRmax" rule is a population approximation; age-predicted HRmax is itself an average (E8).
- Wrist optical HR is acceptably accurate for steady running (E9, E10), less so for intervals (latency, E10)
  and lifting.
- Keeping most running (~70–80%) below the first threshold improved performance more than doing more
  threshold work (E5–E7).
- Pace alone is not the definition of easy in this literature; the plan's 11:45–13:00 /mi range comes from
  a coaching table, not from a measurement on this runner.
- Not found: a validated way to set an individual's easy heart-rate cap without a lab test, other than the
  talk test / RPE.
