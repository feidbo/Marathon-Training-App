# Evidence log: eating to get leaner during marathon training

Gathered Nov 16 2026, before any decision. Source: Europe PMC. ABS = abstract read; FT = full text read.
Not a substitute for a registered dietitian (N1 itself says athletes "should be referred to a registered
dietitian/nutritionist for a personalized nutrition plan").

## How fast to lose

### N2. Garthe I, et al. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 2011;21:97–104 — ABS (RCT, 24 elite athletes)
- Slow loss (0.7% of body weight per week, energy intake −19%) vs fast (1.4%/week, −30%), both with 4
  strength sessions a week. Both lost the same weight overall; the slow group lost more fat (31% vs 21%
  of fat mass) and **gained** 2.1% lean mass, the fast group none. Authors: aim for 0.7%/week.

## Protein

### N3. Hector AJ, Phillips SM. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 2018;28:170–177 — ABS (review)
- "Current recommendations for protein intakes during weight loss in athletes are set at 1.6–2.4 g
  protein/kg/day", higher with a bigger deficit and harder training.

### N4. Helms ER, et al. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 2014;24:127–138 — ABS (systematic review, 6 studies)
- Lean, resistance-trained athletes in a deficit: needs "likely 2.3–3.1 g/kg of FFM [fat-free mass]".

### N5. Jäger R, et al. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2017;14:20 (PMC5477153) — ABS (position stand)
- 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day suffices for most exercising people; per meal about 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g.

## Carbohydrate and fat for endurance training

### N6. Vitale K, Getzin A. Nutrients 2019;11:1289 (PMC6628334) — FT (review; quotes N1)
- The ACSM/AND/DC position stand (N1) "recommends that moderate exercise (1 h/day) requires 5–7 g per
  kilogram of bodyweight per day of CHO, while moderate to high intensity exercise (1–3 h/day) mandates
  6–10 g/kg/day."
- Protein for athletes 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day (N1); 0.3 g/kg within 0–2 h after exercise.
- Fat: "Do not restrict to <20% total caloric energy".
- A low-carbohydrate diet "restricts that athlete's ability to train and race [at] higher intensities".
- During runs over 60 min: 30–60 g carbohydrate per hour.

### N1. Thomas DT, Erdman KA, Burke LM. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2016;48:543–568 — ABS only (position stand)
- Numbers above are taken from N6's quotation of it, not from its full text.

## The risk: eating too little while training

### N7. Mountjoy M, et al. Br J Sports Med 2023;57:1073–1097 — ABS (IOC consensus on REDs)
- Low energy availability ("inadequate energy intake in relation to exercise energy expenditure") causes
  "deleterious health and performance outcomes" in "female and male athletes".

### N8. Cupka M, Sedliak M. Eur J Transl Myol 2023;33:11104 (PMC10388605) — FT (mini-review, male endurance athletes)
- Energy availability = (energy eaten − exercise energy) ÷ fat-free mass. Below 30 kcal per kg fat-free
  mass per day for five days disrupted hormones in laboratory studies. In male endurance athletes low
  energy availability "correlates with decreased testosterone levels, decreased bone density and also
  Resting Metabolic Rate". Smart-watch calorie estimates can be off by up to 600 kcal a day.

## Estimating energy needs

### N9. Frankenfield D, et al. J Am Diet Assoc 2005;105:775–789 — ABS (systematic review)
- The Mifflin-St Jeor equation was the most reliable of four, within 10% of measured resting metabolic
  rate in more people than the others. It needs sex, age, height and weight.

### N10. Brueckner JC, et al. Eur J Appl Physiol 1991;62:385–389 — ABS (10 amateur runners)
- Net energy cost of running: about 171–175 ml of oxygen per kg per km, rising late in runs of 32–42 km.
  At the standard ~5 kcal per litre of oxygen (my conversion, not in the abstract) that is about
  0.87 kcal per kg per km above resting: roughly 117 kcal per mile at 184 lb.

## When to eat (added Nov 17 2026)

### T1. Helms ER, Aragon AA, Fitschen PJ. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2014;11:20 (PMC4033492) — ABS (review)
- Lose "approximately 0.5 to 1%/wk to maximize muscle retention". "alterations in nutrient timing and
  frequency appear to have little effect on fat loss or lean mass retention." Three to six meals a day.

### T2. Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA, Krieger JW. Nutr Rev 2015;73:69–82 — ABS (meta-analysis, 15 studies)
- More frequent meals looked better for fat mass, but "the positive findings were the product of a single
  study, casting doubt".

### T3. Schoenfeld BJ, et al. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2014;11:54 (PMC4242477) — ABS (RCT, 20 women, 4 weeks)
- Fasted vs fed aerobic exercise on a calorie deficit: both lost fat, "no significant between-group
  differences".

### T4. Fernandes-Alves D, et al. Nutr Rev 2026;84:463–486 — ABS (meta-analysis, 30 RCTs, adults with overweight/obesity)
- Time-restricted eating vs the same calories without a window: fat mass −1.50 kg (CI −2.77 to −0.24) but
  also fat-free mass −0.41 kg (CI −0.79 to −0.03). Not athletes.

### T5. Jäger R, et al. (N5 above) — ABS
- Protein doses of 20–40 g "should ideally be evenly distributed, every 3–4 h, across the day".

### Carbohydrate around runs: see N6 (30–60 g/h on runs over an hour; protein within 2 h after).

## Not found / not checked
- No trial of fat loss during a first-marathon build specifically.
- No sourced body-fat percentage at which abdominal muscles become visible; it varies between people.
- Fridge/freezer storage times in the app are standard food-safety guidance recalled from memory, not
  re-checked against a source in this log.
- No evidence gathered on specific foods, supplements, meal timing beyond the above, or on whether a
  deficit slows calf-strain recovery.
- No sourced activity factor for daily living outside training: the plan's multiplier (resting rate × 1.4)
  is an assumption, to be corrected from the weekly weigh-in.
