# Evidence log: calf strain — presentation, rehabilitation and return to running

## Context

The runner reports a tight, stiff right calf that is a little painful on runs and very painful to foam
roll. This log collects what clinical sources say about calf muscle strain injuries (CMSI). It does not
diagnose this runner.

## Questions

1. How do calf strains present (gastrocnemius vs soleus)?
2. What are the rehabilitation stages and exercises, and when do stretching and massage fit in?
3. What criteria show readiness to run and to return to full training?
4. Can training continue with some pain (pain monitoring)?
5. Re-check of claims made in an earlier answer (which relied on abstracts / search excerpts).

## Search

Europe PMC: calf / soleus / gastrocnemius strain with rehabilitation / return to play / risk factors;
pain-monitoring model. Full texts read where open access.

## Sources

### K1. Green B, McClelland JA, Semciw AI, Schache AG, McCall A, Pizzari T. Sports Med Open 2022;8:10 (PMC8761182) — FT
Qualitative interview study of **20 expert clinicians** (elite sport / clinician-researchers). Expert
opinion, not a trial; "there is a dearth of research to guide clinicians dealing with these injuries."

**Presentation.** Gastrocnemius injuries are usually well defined ("shotgun" pain). For soleus, "poorly
localised 'tightness' or 'cramping' that impedes function and does not resolve was pathognomonic";
"symptoms and impairments are at times absent or non-specific until subacute examination", e.g. "'I
thought I was a bit tight,' 'hang on I'm still a bit tight' … 'actually, this feels a bit sore now'".
Look-alikes: DOMS, other lower-leg muscle strains, Achilles tear/tendinopathy; "Bony, neural or medical
causes were perceived to be uncommon"; lumbar spine and neural tension examined.

**Prognosis.** "What trumps MRI is the clinical progression: the recovery of strength, the recovery of
range of motion, the ability to progress through clinical milestones…" Early markers: pain-free walking,
palpation tenderness, stretch tolerance, single-leg calf raise strength, plyometric function.

**Six management phases**, each with load progressions. Key principles quoted:
- Early loading: "The most important thing is getting therapeutic loading started as soon as possible."
  Isometrics ("isometric loading at certain angles … is less symptomatic in the early phase") and calf
  raise variations; single-leg calf raises "as soon as tolerated" — "isometric … then small range
  isotonics, then full range as soon as they can, even if they can only do 2." Multiple loading bouts per
  day and progressive loading through full range were described.
- Loaded strengthening after an early single-leg calf raise benchmark; "soleus load tolerance was perceived
  to be essential for all CMSI prior to introducing dynamic exercises"; knee-straight and knee-bent loading.
- Power/plyometrics after strength benchmarks: vertical first (e.g. single-leg pogos), then horizontal.
- Running: readiness rests on "Strength, hopping capacity and the absence of other clinical signs and
  symptoms"; "running prematurely was cited as the leading cause of early recurrent CMSI". One expert:
  "If you can't do 20 repetitions of single leg hopping without having symptoms there is no way you should
  go running."
- Six running "rules of thumb": "(1) initially run on alternate days, (2) avoid 'plodding' early, (3) do
  not progress volume and intensity on consecutive days, (4) schedule off-field exercises (e.g. loaded
  strengthening) after running, (5) shape running progressions to meet the demands of the sport—don't
  overshoot with excessive volume, (6) avoid sudden changes in conditions, such as the surface and
  footwear." Prolonged slow continuous running ("plodding") early "had been found to predispose to
  recurrence for CMSI involving soleus". For soleus injuries in sports with large running volumes (they
  name distance runners), endurance is "the last thing we tick off".
- Calves give little warning before recurrence: "Calves … feel 'good, good, good' and then 'no good.'"

**Return-to-play checklist (Table 4):** symptoms 0/10 (pain, tightness, cramping); palpation tenderness
0/10; weight-bearing dorsiflexion (knee-to-wall) and straight-leg stretch asymmetry ≤ 10%; **single-leg
calf raise test from the floor: ≥ 30 repetitions, asymmetry ≤ 10%** (screening table: metronome 30
beats/min, to fatigue); loaded strength benchmarks knee-straight and knee-bent; power tests asymmetry
≤ 10%; reconditioned running; ≥ 1 full training session.

Massage, foam rolling and stretching as treatments were not discussed as recommendations in this paper
(stretch *tolerance* appears only as a sign to monitor).

### K2. Halabchi F, Tavana MM, Seifi V, Mahmoudi Zarandi M. Med J Islam Repub Iran 2024;38:55 (PMC11469716) — FT
Narrative review proposing a 4-phase protocol for medial gastrocnemius strain; the authors say the
rehabilitation literature is "few and sparse" and the protocol summarizes "different findings and
opinions" (expert-opinion level). Non-operative management "approximately 3 to 6 weeks".
- Phase 1 (first days): POLICE; "Soft tissue techniques such as mobilization and massage are
  contraindicated at this stage, as they may cause bleeding"; "Stretching is prohibited in this phase";
  isometric plantarflexion at different angles, then non-weight-bearing isotonic; double-leg calf raises
  (knee straight or bent) if tolerated. Progress when: swelling resolved, pain-free range, pain-free
  isometrics, pain-free normal walking.
- Phase 2: soft-tissue techniques allowed; "nonweightbearing, low load, high duration, static stretching
  with a towel"; double- and single-leg calf raises, progressing to loaded. Progress when: full-strength
  plantarflexion without pain, brisk walking without pain.
- Phase 3: weight-bearing wall-lean stretches knee straight and bent; concentric and eccentric loading;
  progress when able to "Jog or prform [sic] light running activities without pain".
- Phase 4: plyometrics, return to running ("slow running exercises on flat surfaces … gradually"),
  "A gradual 5% increase in duration, frequency, and intensity"; before running in sport: no signs or
  symptoms, normal calf strength, normal hopping.
- Return to play: symptoms (pain, tightness, cramps) resolved; tenderness resolved; strength, power,
  endurance and dorsiflexion "with less than 10% asymmetry compared with the uninjured side".

### K3. Pagan-Rosado R, et al. Sports Med Open 2025;11:158 (PMC12701110) — FT
Narrative review (imaging/grading focus). "Muscle rest is critical during the initial 3–5 days following
injury"; non-selective NSAIDs "generally not recommended within the first 24–72 h"; gentle active ROM and
isometrics early. "Early-loading strategies most often begin with 2–3 weeks of light calf stretches and
isometric single-leg calf raises"; single-leg heel raises, then eccentric strengthening knee straight and
bent; progressive loading. Mild–moderate injuries: "accelerated rehabilitation course (2–6 weeks)".
"Clinical factors remain the gold standard" for return; "Reinjury risk is highest within the first 2 months".
Red flags to review: DVT triad "worsening pain, swelling, and tenderness" and acute compartment syndrome
signs. Note: K3 allows light stretching early, whereas K2 prohibits stretching in phase 1 — the sources
disagree.

### K4. Green B, Pizzari T. Br J Sports Med 2017;51:1189–1194 — ABS (systematic review, 10 studies, 5397 athletes)
"Increasing age and previous calf strain injury are the most predictive of future calf injury."

### K5. Silbernagel KG, et al. Am J Sports Med 2007;35:897–906 — ABS (RCT, 38 patients)
**Achilles tendinopathy**, not muscle strain: continuing running and jumping "with the use of a
pain-monitoring model" during a progressive calf/tendon loading programme showed "No negative effects"
versus 6 weeks of active rest. The model's pain thresholds are in the full text, which was not accessible,
so no thresholds are recorded here. Applicability to muscle strains is not established, and K1 warns calf
strains give little warning before recurrence.

| --- | --- |
| Age and previous calf strain are the strongest risk factors | Confirmed (K4) |
| Rehab goes isometric → isotonic → heavier/faster → running | Confirmed (K1, K2, K3) |

| "35 single-leg raises bent and straight, ~80% symmetry" (JOSPT 2025 soccer paper) | **Not verified** — not indexed in Europe PMC and the journal site refused automated access. Not used. The verified benchmark is ≥ 30 single-leg raises, ≤ 10% asymmetry (K1). |

| Don't stretch a strained calf early | Mixed: K2 prohibits in phase 1; K3 describes light stretching early |

## Not found

- No randomized trial of calf-strain rehabilitation protocols; all management guidance here is expert
  opinion or narrative review.
- No calf-strain-specific evidence on foam rolling.
