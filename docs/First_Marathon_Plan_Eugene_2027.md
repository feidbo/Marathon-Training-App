# First Marathon Plan: Eugene 2027

## Overview

This plan builds you from about 8 miles a week to a 41-mile peak week and a 20-mile long run, ready for the Eugene Marathon on Sunday, April 25, 2027. It runs 191 days from Friday, October 16, 2026, plus race day.

4:30 is an ambitious goal at your current fitness. From a 2:10–2:20 half, the Riegel formula predicts 4:31–4:52, and for recreational runners Riegel came out at least 10 minutes too fast for half of marathoners (Vickers & Vertosick 2016). Your March 7 half marathon sets your race pace; the table in Week 20 shows how.

**The long-run rule**

No run is more than 10% longer than your longest run in the previous 30 days. In a study of 5,205 runners, runs 10–30% longer than that came with a 64% higher injury rate (Frandsen et al. 2025). That is why long runs grow in small steps, reaching 20 miles three weeks before the race. If your longest run in the month before October 16 was under 3.5 miles, make your first long run no more than 10% longer than it and build from there.

Before March 7, if 10:18 feels harder than 5–6 out of 10 on marathon-pace runs, run them by effort instead. Most of your running, about 80%, is easy; judge it by effort, since hills, heat and fatigue all slow you down.

**Ground rules**

- Never move a missed run onto Tuesday. Skip it and carry on.
- Missed under a week: resume where you are. Missed 1–2 weeks: repeat the last full week before moving on. Either way, recheck the long-run rule against what you actually ran in the last 30 days.
- Pain that changes your stride, or gets worse as you run, ends the run that day.
- On strength days, lift first and then run, or lift later in the day.

## Strength program

Strength is one machine routine: six exercises in two blocks, twice a week through February 7, then once a week. It works the glutes, quads, hamstrings and calves, plus your back and arms with one assisted pull-up.

Heavy leg work generally makes running cheaper: you use less energy at the same pace (Støren 2008; Blagrove 2018). Whether it prevents running injuries is less certain. Across many sports, strength training cut injuries to under a third (Lauersen 2014), but a trial in first-time New York City marathoners found a self-directed program did not reduce injuries that stopped training (Toresdahl 2020).

**How to run a session**

- Lift first, then do the day's easy run, or run later in the day. Lifting before endurance work gave slightly larger leg-strength gains (Eddens 2018).
- Start with one light warm-up set on the leg press and the hip thrust.
- Block 1: one set of leg press, then calf raises, then assisted pull-ups, then rest. Repeat for the listed sets; skip an exercise once its sets are done.
- Block 2: one set each of hip thrust, hip abduction and leg curl, then rest. Repeat for the listed sets.
- Rest 60–90 s between rounds in Phase 1, and 90 s–2 min in Phases 2 and 3.

| Exercise | Machine | Phase 1: Oct 16 – Dec 13, Mon + Thu | Phase 2: Dec 14 – Feb 7, Mon + Thu | Phase 3: Feb 8 – Apr 5, once a week |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1a. Leg press | Leg press | 2 × 12 | 3 × 6 | 2 × 5 |
| 1b. Calf raise | Same leg press, balls of feet on the platform edge | 2 × 15 | 2 × 10 | 1 × 10 |

Phase 3 drops to one session a week because one heavy session holds strength (Spiering 2021) and frees energy for the biggest running weeks. It falls on Mondays, except March 8, the day after the half, when it moves to Thursday, March 11. March 1 and April 12 are light sessions, the last heavy session is Monday, April 5, and there is no lifting in race week.

**Form cues**

- Leg press: feet about shoulder-width; lower under control to about 90 degrees at the knee; don't lock your knees at the top.
- Calf raise on the leg press: keep the safety stops engaged; lower slowly through a full range.
- Assisted pull-up: more counterweight means more help. Set it so the last two reps are hard, pull your chest toward the bar, and lower slowly. Use less counterweight as you get stronger.
- Hip thrust: chin tucked, push through your heels, pause one second at the top.
- Add weight when every set feels easier than its target effort.

### Daily kneeling quad stretch (3 minutes)

Kneel on a pad with one knee down and the other foot flat in front. Reach back, hold the back ankle, and draw that heel toward your glute. Tuck your pelvis under and keep your hips square; you should feel it in the front of the thigh and hip.

Hold 90 seconds per side, every day including rest days. Do it after runs or in the evening, not right before a run, and back off the depth if your knee complains. Stretching improves range of motion but has not been shown to prevent injuries, and holding a stretch 60 seconds or more just before running can briefly reduce performance (Behm 2016).

## Taper and race (Apr 5 – Apr 25)

The taper runs three weeks. Mileage drops about 20%, then about 35%, then about 70% in race week, while marathon-pace work and five running days a week stay in. The last two weeks average about a 54% cut, inside the 41–60% range a meta-analysis found works best when intensity and frequency are kept (Bosquet 2007).

## Fueling, injury prevention, and adjusting the plan

### Fueling

Practice fueling on long runs from January 10 so race-day fueling feels routine by March. The gut adapts with training, improving how quickly carbohydrate leaves the stomach and how comfortable it feels (Jeukendrup 2017).

| When | Carbohydrate target | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long runs of 8.25–11.5 mi (Jan 10 – Feb 7) | About 30 g every 45 min | Gels are usually 20–25 g; check the label |
| Long runs of 12.5–15.5 mi (Feb 14 – Mar 7) | 45–60 g per hour | Start early in the run |
| Long runs of 17 mi and up (from Mar 14), and race day | 60–90 g per hour | Above about 60 g per hour, use products that mix glucose and fructose |
| Friday and Saturday before the race | 10–12 g per kg of body weight per day | Spread across meals and snacks; choose foods your gut handles well |
| Race morning | 1–4 g per kg, 1–4 hours before the start | A breakfast you tested before long runs |

These targets follow the joint sports-nutrition position statement (2016) and Jeukendrup (2014). Drink when thirsty rather than on a schedule; drinking too much is the main cause of dangerously low blood sodium in endurance events (Hew-Butler 2015). Use the same gels and drinks on long runs that you will use in Eugene.

### Staying injury-free

No plan can guarantee you stay injury-free, but these habits have the best evidence behind them:

- Follow the long-run rule: no run more than 10% longer than your longest in the past 30 days (Frandsen 2025).
- Keep up the strength work. It clearly improves running economy; its effect on running injuries is less certain (see the science table).
- Rotate two or more pairs of running shoes. Runners who did had a 39% lower injury rate in one study (Malisoux 2015).
- Get pain that changes your stride, or lasts more than a few days, checked by a physical therapist or doctor.

### Adjusting the plan

- **Sick:** with mild cold symptoms only, a shorter easy run is a common rule of thumb. With fever, chest symptoms or body aches, don't run until you are well; see a doctor if unsure.
- **Missed under a week:** carry on from today's date.
- **Missed 1–2 weeks:** repeat the last full week you completed, then continue.
- **Missed more than 2 weeks before March:** drop back two weeks and recheck the goal at the half.
- **After any missed time:** recheck the long-run rule against what you actually ran in the last 30 days.
- **Signs of doing too much:** easy pace feeling hard for several days, lasting fatigue, poor sleep, or a resting heart rate that stays above your normal. Take an extra rest day and make the next week lighter.
- **Swaps:** you can swap Saturday and Sunday in any week, but never put two hard days back to back.

## The science behind the plan

Each choice below was checked on October 14, 2026 against the paper, its abstract, or a published summary of it. Where the evidence is weak or mixed, the table says so.

Observational and exploratory. | [Frandsen et al., 2025, Br J Sports Med 59:1203–1210](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/How-much-running-is-too-much/991144502402621) |
